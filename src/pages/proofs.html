A model can draft a detection rule faster than anybody can check it. A proof is the check, made by a program rather than by the author, and written down so whoever receives the rule — a reviewer, CI, a customer — can rely on it without trusting whoever handed it over.

Three verdicts

VerdictMeans
provenevery check passed on evidence that could have failed it
refuteda check failed, and the proof names what
inconclusivenothing failed, but nothing could have: a rule checked only against its own fixtures

A refutation is signed like a proof: what did not work is what the next attempt most needs to know.

Prove a detection rule

aertun content keygen --out root.key     # once: a signing key in the store; move root.key off the machine
aertun proof detection --rule rule.json --corpus corpus.jsonl \
    --key .aertun/content/release.key --public-key proof-key.json \
    --out rule.proof.json --by you@example.com

It checks, in order:

  1. valid — the rule is a rule; an invalid one is refuted and nothing else runs.
  2. fixtures — its own must-match and must-not-match examples hold. Necessary, never enough.
  3. corpus — over a labelled corpus (one JSON object a line, as aertun detect replay reads), it fires on every entry labelled as its own and on nothing else it reads. A false positive somebody recorded and nobody fixed still refutes it.

Recall and specificity are reported as 95% Wilson lower bounds, so a proof over one example reads as the weak thing it is. Without a corpus the best verdict is inconclusive. --model and --prompt-sha256 record which model wrote the rule and a digest of its prompt — never the prompt.

Verify one

aertun proof verify --key proof-key.json --subject rule.json rule.proof.json

Both signatures must hold against a key you were handed, never one the document supplies; --subject checks the proof is about exactly that file. It exits non-zero on anything but proven, so it is a CI gate.

Certify the analyst's threshold

aertun analyst calibrate --key signing.key --public-key proof-key.json --out calibration.json \
    --epsilon 0.05 --delta 0.05
aertun analyst triage --calibration calibration.json --calibration-key proof-key.json

The findings verified people have ruled are put to the configured model, and the lowest threshold is fitted at which closing suggestions on findings people ruled real stay under --epsilon with confidence one minus --delta — an exact Clopper–Pearson bound, corrected for every threshold examined. The certificate names the model, the adapter and the prompt template by digest, and expires. Triage gates on it only when it verifies and fits; one that does not, and any answer not scored on the channel it was fitted on, goes to a person rather than back to the declared threshold.

Score another vendor's AI

aertun verdict import charlotte.csv --source charlotte
aertun verdict prove --source charlotte --since 2026-10-01 --tolerance 0.01 \
    --key signing.key --public-key proof-key.json --out charlotte.proof.json

The export carries the columns alert, verdict and at, and optionally confidence, disposition, disposition_at and source; a file with any other column, such as the product's reasoning, is refused whole. Each verdict is joined to the decision a verified person made on the finding that alert raised here — the source's mapping names the path its alert id is at as alert — and only verdicts given before the decision count. The claim is about false closures, alerts the product closed that a person found real, against a tolerance you declare first: refuted when the exact lower bound is over it, naming the alerts; proven when the upper bound is at or under it on at least 30 closures; inconclusive between. The rate is over the closures somebody decided, which are not a random sample, so the proof states that coverage, and how many verdicts were delivered here before the decision rather than resting on the product's own clock.

Judge closures again, blind

aertun adjudicate size --rate 0.01                 # how many closures a target needs: 299
aertun adjudicate draw --source charlotte --since 2026-10-01 --per 299
aertun adjudicate next SAMPLE                       # or the console's Adjudication screen
aertun adjudicate judge SAMPLE --item 4 --judgement real --because "…"
aertun adjudicate prove SAMPLE --tolerance 0.01 --key signing.key --out adjudication.proof.json

A verdicts proof counts the closures somebody happened to decide, and people look again at what looked wrong. Here a sample is drawn at random from every closure the product's AI made in the period, reviewed or not, and from every closure people made, and its seed and digests are committed to the signed audit log before any item is shown; anybody holding the seed and the population can recompute the selection. Each item is shown with its evidence and nothing that says who closed it, how, or which of the two it came from. Nobody who closed an item may judge it, each is judged once, agents may not see the queue, and a judgement is a record in the log, never a decision on the finding — a product that learns from analysts cannot learn the answers.

The proof reads the AI's share judged real against the tolerance, as a verdicts proof does, and reports the people's share beside it. It is refused unless every item of the committed sample was judged after the commitment by a verified person, and it says how many samples of the same product were drawn before it.

Hold an agent to its own account

aertun agent maps triage-bot --host build01      # once: where it runs
aertun agent reconcile triage-bot --since 2026-11-01 --telemetry triage-bot \
    --key signing.key --public-key proof-key.json --out triage-bot.proof.json

The agent's account is the tool calls its GenAI telemetry reported, under the identity --telemetry declares (its gen_ai.agent.id; the registered name by default). It is held against what Aertun saw without asking it: every call the agent interface answered for the agent, from the signed audit log once the log verifies, and every connection the recorder saw leave a machine declared to run that agent and no other. A call is matched to the agent's report of the same tool within --window (two minutes by default), however its host prefixed the tool's name.

refuted names what is unaccounted for: a call it did not report, a call it reports and the server never received, a connection while it reported nothing. An agent that reported nothing while it was seen acting is refuted, and the proof says it cannot tell that from one never instrumented. proven needs at least ten observations with nothing unaccounted for; inconclusive is fewer. A machine declared for several agents, or under a name a proof cannot carry, is counted and left out; what the agent did in other systems is not covered, because nothing ties it to its identity there.

Kept proofs, and the Proofs screen

aertun proof keep rule.proof.json --public-key proof-key.json
aertun proof list [--kind detection|calibration|verdicts|reconciliation|adjudication]
aertun proof show ID

verdict prove, analyst calibrate, agent reconcile and adjudicate prove keep the proofs they make; a proof made elsewhere — in CI, on another deployment — is kept with proof keep, or from the Proofs screen under Proving ground, only after it verifies against the key handed in with it. The key is kept beside it by fingerprint: verifying says the key signed it, and whether that key is one to believe is yours to judge. A kept proof is checked again every time it is read, so one edited afterwards is listed as no longer verifying rather than as what it now says.

Listing and reading are what the agent interface offers, as list_proofs and read_proof, every value labelled with whether somebody outside Aertun could have written it. A detection proof's rule name is the submitter's, perhaps the model under test, so the screen marks it untrusted where the agent interface labels it. Keeping a proof is a change, and no agent may make one.

What a proof is

An in-toto Statement in a DSSE envelope: the subject is the rule file by digest; the predicate (https://aertun.github.io/proof/detection/v1) names the claim in words, the verifier and its version, every material it read by digest, each check, and the verdict. It is signed twice, Ed25519 and ML-DSA-65, the second in the context aertun/proof/v1. The Ed25519 half is plain DSSE, so in-toto and Sigstore tooling check it with no help from Aertun. The signing key file must be readable by its owner alone.
